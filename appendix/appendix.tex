\section{Research question and scope}
This project asks whether a context-sensitive target representation distinguishes
same-sense from different-sense uses more effectively than static alternatives.
It compares type-level GloVe, a lexical GloVe context mean, and an unfine-tuned
BERT-base target representation. It is a controlled representation probe, not a
claim about fine-tuned task state of the art.

\section{Representations}
\subsection{Static baselines}
Both static comparisons use the uncased GloVe release with 300 numbers per word.
The target-only comparison looks up the WiC lemma in each sentence. Because the
lemma is the same on both sides, its similarity is always 1 when a vector is
available. GloVe has no vector for 18 training lemmas; we replaced those missing
scores with the median of the available training scores.

For the nearby-word comparison, we averaged the GloVe vectors of words around
the marked target, leaving out the target itself. We tried two, five, and ten
words on either side, as well as all words in the sentence. If none of the
surrounding words had a GloVe vector, we used the target lemma as a fallback.
That happened once in training for each window size and never in validation.

\subsection{Contextual target}
We used the pretrained \path{google-bert/bert-base-uncased} checkpoint without
fine-tuning it. The tokenizer tells us which pieces of text belong to the
marked word. We averaged the BERT vectors for all pieces that overlap that word.
One pass through BERT gave us each of its 12 layers and the average of the last
four layers. We saved these representations for the comparisons below.

\section{Selection and evaluation protocol}
We made every model choice using training data. With random seed 2026, we split
the 5,428 balanced training pairs into 4,342 pairs for tuning and 1,086 for an
internal check, keeping the same class balance in both groups. We chose a
similarity cutoff for each candidate on the tuning group using macro F1. Then
we compared candidates on the internal group, using macro F1 first, followed by
accuracy, ROC-AUC, and the simpler representation if needed. We fixed the
winners before looking at validation results and refitted their cutoffs on all
5,428 training pairs.

\section{Error analysis}
The groups above do not overlap. On 54 of the 106 pairs missed by both main
systems, the target-only GloVe diagnostic was also wrong; on the other 52 it
happened to be right. BERT missed 210 pairs in total. Thirty-three of those
scores were within 0.025 of its cutoff, while 53 were in the quarter of errors
furthest from the cutoff.

BERT recovered 77.43\% of same-meaning pairs and 56.74\% of different-meaning
pairs. Nearby-word GloVe recovered 46.39\% and 64.58\%, respectively. In saved
examples, BERT placed a same-meaning anatomical \emph{ball}/\emph{balls} pair
far apart, but placed different meanings of \emph{engrave} and \emph{map} close
together. It also linked the same meaning in \emph{rust remover} and \emph{paint
remover}, despite their different wording. Fine differences in meaning may
explain some errors, but these examples alone cannot establish the cause.

\section{Reproducibility record}

\section{Limitations}
We tested one English dataset, so the result may change on other tasks or
languages. Our intervals reflect variation among these validation pairs, not
uncertainty from the dataset design or our choice of models. We used an older
frozen BERT base checkpoint. Our simple GloVe context average does not model
word order or syntax. A single similarity cutoff may also miss useful detail.
We did not score the test split because its labels are unavailable.
